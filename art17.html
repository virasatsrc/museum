THE FIRST ANGLO-SIKH WAR (1845-46)

On 18 December 1845, the First Anglo-Sikh War commenced with the Battle of Mudki. The Sikhs under Raja Lal Singh were smaller in number but lost an initial advantage when their General deserted them and retreated to his camp at Ferozeshah. But the leaderless Sikh Soldiers refused to give in and fought a grim hand-to-hand battle against the British forces. While the Sikhs had to finally retreat from Mudki, the battle was an unpleasant surprise for the British - they suffered 872 fatal and non-fatal casualties, losing few of their top military commanders.

The next battle was fought at Ferozeshah on 21-22 December 1845. The Sikh Army won the first phase of battle but was again let down by two traitorous commanders, who refused to reinforce its initial success. Despite the retreat of their generals, the Sikhs fought on leaderless but had to eventually retreat.

The British Army followed up its victory at Ferozeshah by moving up the Sutlej River and advancing on to Lahore. During the advance, a Sikh Army under Ranjodh Singh Majithia attacked the rear echelons of the British Army at Buddowal (Battle of Buddowal, 21 January 1846), leading to heavy losses to the British Army - their baggage train and stores were captured by the Sikhs. But due to scarcity of troops and resources, the Sikhs withdrew from Buddowal and took up defences at Aliwal village on the banks of Sutlej River. Here, another decisive battle (Battle of Aliwal, 28 January 1846) was fought under Ranjodh Singh Majithia. Once again, when their commander deserted his troops, the Sikhs refused to retreat and fought with desperate valour. Large number of soldiers were killed in battle and many were pushed into the river and drowned.

The last battle of the First Anglo-Sikh War was fought at Sobraon on 10 February 1846. When many Sikh commanders began to flee in the thick of battle, the troops under Sham Singh Attariwala took up their last stand and were killed in the battle. Approximately 10,000 Sikhs were killed in action at Sobraon which was a crushing defeat for the Lahore Durbar. Lord Hardinge who saw the action wrote, "Few escaped; no one, it may be said, surrendered. The Sikhs met their fate with the resignation which distinguished their race." The British Commander-in-Chief at Sobraon, General Sir Hugh Gough described the battle as the Waterloo of India.

Brief Interlude. The British victory in the First Anglo-Sikh War was followed up with a series of steps to further their consolidation in Punjab. On 08 March 1846, the Treaty of Lahore, embodying the clauses of the partial annexation of Punjab was signed and on 16 March 1846, Jammu and Kashmir was carved out of Punjab as a separate kingdom. The initial two battalions of the Sikh Regiment (Ferozepore and Loodianah Sikhs) were also raised during July-August 1846. The British further consolidated their hold over Punjab through another Treaty of Lahore on 16 December 1846. At the same time, the Sikh Army under Sher Singh Attariwala decided to hold the British by taking up defences on the Chenab River, which led to another series of battles, collectively known as the Second Anglo-Sikh War.
