Title: Battle of Gallipoli (1915)
Category: Battle Gallery
Regiment: Sikh Regiment

During the Gallipoli campaign between April 1915 to January 1916 in the First World War, a joint British and French Operation was mounted to capture the Ottoman capital of Constantinople, and secure a sea route to Russia. On 26 April 1915, British and French forces suffered disastrously from the determined counter-attacks by the Turks on their earlier beach

landing at Cape Helles, at the Southern tip of the Gallipoli Peninsula, in the Ottoman Empire. General Sir lan Hamilton, Commander-in-Chief of the Mediterranean Expeditionary Force, determined that the British and French should try to take two strategic inland points, the village of Krithia, and a neighbouring hill, Achi Baba, from where they could push North. The Allies were sent into battle on 28 April 1915, to which the Turkish forces resisted strongly. Allied planning was uncoordinated, and the attacks were poorly led.

The Allies suffered heavy losses, and were forced to abandon the attacks after 10 hours. It was at this stage that XIV SIKHS, as part of the 29th Indian Brigade, was detached from Egypt as reinforcement, and arrived at Cape Helles, on 30 April 1915. XIV SIKHS had moved into trenches facing Krithia, surviving the misery of the first night, in wet and broken trenches, with Turkish snipers inflicting casualties. The Second Battle of Krithia was fought between 06-08 May, during which about 600 yards were gained. The battle maintained the front from 9 May till relieved, showing great resilience. XIV SIKHS were tasked to relieve the 89th PUNJABIS, the right flanking unit to the Gurkhas, and also two companies of the 6th GURKHAS, despite not having seen very active action. The battle lost three British Officers and 78 All Ranks, as a consequence of devastating Turkish artillery shelling, and sniping by small arms.

All British attacks were from the front, and none succeeded, except in altering the frontline by perhaps a few hundred yards. The front was then advanced by digging a forward line by night, abandoning it in the day, and reoccupying it on the subsequent night. This led to some of these forward lines being occupied by the Turks, which had to be recaptured at considerable cost. On 04 June, full-scale attacks were launched in two waves, with the objective of first capturing, and then passing over the Turkish line. A mixed force of XIV SIKHS (Half Battalion), Gurkhas (Half Battalion) and the LANCASHIRE FUSILIERS formed part of the first wave of the brigade attacks. The assault was meticulously planned to the minute, but the end result was the capture of a single enemy trench, at the cost of 82 per cent casualties of the total strength launched into attack. In this battle, XIV SIKHS lost 371 officers and men, killed or wounded. It was the highest sacrifice after the Battle of Samana Ridge. On the morning of 04 June, artillery opened up, but had little effect on the enemy.

At noon, the first wave of the Fusiliers dashed forward which was mowed down by the enemy fire and their advance was held up. The Gurkhas on the left gained a little ground, but were forced to withdraw to their original line on the face of very heavy Machine Gun fire, hidden on both sides of the ravine. Two British officers were killed, but the Sikhs rushed on and captured the first line of the enemy trenches. Havildar Maghar Singh (later Subedar Major and decorated with the OBI), particularly showed courage and determination during the assault. The unit suffered very heavy casualties, and was reduced to a fraction in a very short time. The second wave of the attacks, comprising Number 1 and No 3 Companies and the Battalion Headquarters, was launched at 1215 hrs. This wave went through to the enemy's third line of trenches and held on to it till the morning of 05 June, when troops had to be withdrawn as they were being encircled. By then, the CO, the Medical Officer and only 47 men were left unwounded. The Regiment Camp was being guarded by the carpenters, armourers and boot-makers.

Over the next two days, the total number of survivors rose to three British Officers and 134 VCOs / Sepoys, from the assaulting strength of 15 Officers and 574 VCOs / Sepoys. It was sheer slaughter, but there were words of great praise from General Hamilton. The Secretary of State for India, paid a glowing tribute to the Sikh Battalion in the House of Commons. A new offensive was launched at the end of June. XIV SIKHS, now reduced to skeleton strength, still acted as reserve to the 29th Division. Although General Sir lan Hamilton was neither able to achieve the assured victory envisaged, nor to beat back the frontal attack, he was full of admiration for the Sikhs.

The Battle of Sari Bair, also known as the August Offensive, was the final attempt made by the British in August 1915, to seize control of the Gallipoli Peninsula, from the Ottoman Empire. XIV SIKHS took part in the Battle of Sari Bair as part of the Indian Brigade, under Field Marshal Birdwood, commanding the Allied Forces, and formed part of the left assaulting column. XIV SIKHS was allotted to the Northern column, as part of 29th Indian Brigade, which along with 4th Australian Brigade, were to capture 'Hill Q' and Koja Chemen Tepe. The battle got over by the end of December 1915, after having suffered an enormous number of casualties (27 British Officers, 20 VCOs and 1000 men), after which, XIV SIKHS moved to Egypt. XIV SIKHS left Gallipoli with a great reputation, and their gallantry and devotion to duty was recognized by the award of 35 Indian Distinguished Service Medals (IDSM).
