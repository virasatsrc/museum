VIRASAT : Historical Legacy of Sikh Regimental Centre

The Sikh Regimental Centre traces its origins to Sep 1921, when XXXV Sikh stationed at Jhelum (now in Pakistan) was converted into a Training Centre. Col JC Freeland, CB, CBE was the first Commandant. The first Colours were presented by HRH The Prince of Wales in Feb 1922 at Lahore.

Due to inadequate training/admin infrastructure, the Centre moved to Nowshera, NWFP where it remained till Sep 1947. Upon Partition, SRC moved to Ambala and Col MM Badshah became the first Indian Officer as Commandant.

During the Indo-Pak War 1947-48, the Centre urgently trained recruits for 1 Sikh, 2 Sikh & 7 Sikh, while raising new battalions. It also served as refuge for Sikh troops and families rendered homeless. Between 11-15 Mar 1952, SRC moved to Meerut. Post 1962 War, large expansions took place with new Sikh battalions raised.

After 24 years at Meerut, SRC made its fourth move to Ramgarh Cantt in Apr 1976 under Col BB Upreti, AVSM, VSM. Located on the banks of the Damodar in 795 acres, the cantonment traces back to Mar 1941 when Italian POWs were interned here. The 22nd & 38th Div of Chinese Expeditionary Force (KMT) were reformed here between 1942-45 for the Burma Campaign.

During the move to Ramgarh, the War Memorial with 30 marble pillars & 'Nanak Shahi' bricks was dismantled piece by piece and re-assembled at Ramgarh on 16 Mar 1977. Foundation stone was laid by Col of Regt Maj Gen RT Morlin, AVSM. The first Platoon (19th Pl) was attested on 24th May 1976 with marked fanfare. Since then, SRC has produced soldiers who have met every challenge with distinction. For its exceptional work and performance, SRC was awarded the 'GOC-in-C, CC Unit Appreciation' on 15 Jan 2015.

VIRASAT stands as a tribute to the Sikh Regiment's ethos — "Nischay Kar Apni Jeet Karon". The museum houses Colours, war trophies, uniforms, weapons, photographs, and personal memorabilia that chronicle over a century of sacrifice, from the World Wars to modern conflicts. It serves as an inspiration for recruits and a pilgrimage for veterans, embodying the Regiment's repository of history, valour, and heritage.

History of Ramgarh

The history of Ramgarh can be traced back to the Lower Paleolithic age, with the discovery of tools found along the Damodar River. The area falls within the Chotanagpur Plateau which during the Mahajanapada period was under the jurisdiction of King Jarasandh. During the Mauryan Empire, King Ashoka took control of the entire Chotanagpur region and the region subsequently was strongly influenced by the Buddhist and Jain cultures. On his Deccan conquest, King Samudragupta of the Gupta empire, passed through this area.

During the mid-fourteenth century, the 'Ramgarh Raj' was established in 1368 whose founder king was Raja Bagdeo Singh, with his capital located in Sira. The capital kept shifting to Urda, Badam, Ramgarh and Padma throughout the period and was finally moved back to Ramgarh in 1670.

The East India Company was given rights to administer Ramgarh area by King Shah Alam II in 1771 and Captain Comac was appointed the Military Collector of Ramgarh, whose headquarter was in Chatra. The Military district of Ramgarh contained Nagpur, Palamu, Hazaribagh, Chatra, Giridih and Kodarma. The East India Company troops stationed here were used to suppress the Kol Revolt in 1831-32. After the Revolt was suppressed, many Police Stations were established in the area by the British and Ramgarh was one of them.

In March 1940, the 53rd Session of Indian National Congress (INC) was held at Jhanda Chowk, Ramgarh under the presidency of Maulana Abul Qalam Azad - this site is currently located within the Sikh Regimental Centre opposite the Main Office Complex. Mahatma Gandhi, Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Patel, Dr. Rajendra Prasad, Sarojini Naidu, Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan, Acharya JB Kripalani, Industrialist Jamnalal Bajaj and other leaders of the Indian freedom movement attended the session. Mahatma Gandhi also opened a Khadi and Village Industries Exhibition at Ramgarh.

Ramgarh Cantonment was formally established in March 1941, when Italian PsOW were interned here. As the war against Japan came closer to the frontiers of Eastern India, the Italian PsOW were re-located to Yol Camp and Chinese troops, who had retreated from Burma, were stationed at Ramgarh Cantonment. Between 1942-1945, thousands of Chinese (Kuomintang) were trained and equipped here by American trainers, for military operations against Japan.

The Sikh Regimental Centre was shifted in four Military Special Trains from Meerut to Ramgarh Cantonment in April 1976, where it was allotted 795 Acres of land. The move took place under Colonel BB Upreti, AVSM, VSM, who commanded the Centre till April 1978. Colonel KC Kalley, VSM, the Deputy Commandant relieved him and worked on the Centre's consolidation until July 1981. Since then, Ramgarh Cantonment has the unique distinction of housing two Infantry Regimental Centers within the confines of the same campus.

HISTORY OF SIKH REGIMENT

The Sikh Regiment was raised in 1846 at the end of the First Anglo-Sikh War while its roots are traced directly to the Khalsa Army of Maharaja Ranjit Singh. The initial two battalions designated as "XIV Ferozepore Sikhs" and "XV Ludhiana Sikhs" were raised on 01 August 1846, which was followed by the raising of "XLV Rattray's" in 1856, "XXXVI Sikhs" in 1887 and "XLVII" Sikhs in 1901. A sixth battalion, "XXXV Sikhs" is today the Sikh Regimental Centre (SRC). Presently, the regiment has grown to 21 regular Infantry Battalions, three Rashtriya Rifles Battalions, one SFC Unit and three Territorial Army Battalions. In addition, 4 Mechanised Infantry Battalion (erstwhile XIV Ferozepore Sikhs/1 Sikh), INS Kirpan (a Khukri-class corvette, de-commissioned in 2023) and Number 12 Squadron (Transport), IAF are affiliated to the Sikh Regiment.

Prior to independence in 1947, the Sikh Regiment participated in numerous wars all around the globe such as the Second Opium War in China, the Second Anglo-Afghan War, many campaigns on the North-West Frontier, the Western Front, Gallipoli and Mesopotamia campaigns of the First World War, the Third Anglo-Afghan War and the North African, Italian and Burma campaigns of the Second World War, earning many gallantry awards and battle honours in the process.

Post-Independence, in the Indo-Pakistan War of 1947-1948, 1 Sikh was the first unit to be airlifted to Srinagar on 27 October 1947 to defend the Kashmir Valley from tribal invaders – this landmark event is commemorated as "Infantry Day". Battalions of the Sikh Regiment valiantly fought in the Sino-Indian War of 1962, the Indo-Pakistan War of 1965, the Indo-Pakistan War of 1971 and the Kargil War in 1999, earning many laurels and accolades. The Regiment has earned 106 Battle Honours and 18 Theatre Honours and is among the most decorated Infantry Regiments of the Indian Army.

The Regiment draws its soldiers mostly from the states of Punjab, Haryana and Jammu & Kashmir - the troops are mainly "Jat Sikhs" who are known for their hardihood of character, a spirit of adaptation and fun-loving nature, which is the quintessential essence of a "Saint Soldier", as preached by the 10th Sikh Guru, Sri Guru Gobind Singh.

HAVALDAR ISHAR SINGH, IOM, IDSM

Havildar Ishar Singh was born on 12 September 1858 at Village Jhorran (near Jagraon) in Ludhiana, Punjab, to an agrarian family of Sardar Dulla Singh. Since childhood, he aspired to become a soldier and initially served the Punjab Frontier Force for 11 years. In 1887, he was enrolled into the 36th Sikhs.

Right from the beginning, Ishar had a penchant for active field duties rather than administrative chores in camps. Due to his martial qualities, he developed a formidable military reputation and commanded complete loyalty of his troops.

During the epic Battle of Saragarhi, he gave a heroic account of his leadership skills while fighting an adversary much superior in numbers. Despite all odds, he declined many enemy offers to surrender, assembled his soldiers and inspired them to keep fighting. When nearly half his men were either dead or wounded in the first six hours of fighting, he ordered the rest to leave their loop-holed battlements and engage the enemy in hand-to-hand fighting.

The final moments of his life and leadership are described by military historian Major General James Lunt in Saragarhi: Sikhs and Pathans, 1897 (published in History Today, Volume 27, Issue 04, April 1977):

“Now Ishar Singh knew that the game was up. Returning to the roof, he flashed his last message to Fort Lockhart—the enemy was in the fort, the Sikhs were overrun but would not surrender. Then, placing the heliograph carefully in the case, he went down for the last time to join his men in their final stand. Somewhere in the midst of this holocaust of blood, smoke and flames, Havildar Ishar Singh fell to a Pathan’s knife.”

BATTLE OF SARAGARHI

The Battle of Saragarhi fought by 36th Sikhs (now, 4 SIKH) is one of the greatest saga of collective bravery and courage in the history of warfare. In the year 1897, the tribal gatherings (“Jirgahs”) of NWFP (now, Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa) decided to rise against the British Indian Army garrisoned in various locations of Khyber Region.

The Samana Ridge, a key feature dominating Southern Khyber, was being defended by 36th Sikhs under their Commanding Officer, Lieutenant Colonel John Haughton. This five mile long ridge was dotted with Fort Lockhart on the eastern end and Fort Gulistan on the west. At its center where the ridge reached its highest point, was a rocky outcrop, which intercepted the line of sight between the two forts. The Saragarhi Post was raised at this spot as a “signaling station” between the two forts. All three locations had “heliographs” as the only means of communication. The battalion was almost equally split between the two forts and Saragarhi was lightly manned with 21 Sikh soldiers and one NCE (Non-Combatant Enrolled).

The defenders of Saragarhi were mostly young soldiers (less than five years service) from the Signal Platoon, under the command of Havildar Ishar Singh. On the morning of 12 September 1897, a lashkar of about 12,000 Afridi and Orakzai tribesmen invested Fort Gulistan and cut-off Saragarhi from Fort Lockhart. Thousands of tribesmen then attacked the Saragarhi Post and this message was promptly communicated to Fort Lockhart via Heliograph by a young Signalman, Gurmukh Singh. On being ordered to “hold their position”, the men under Havildar Ishar Singh engaged the enemy from loopholes on the Saragarhi rooftop and later hand-to-hand battle in the post quarters. These warriors were armed with Martini Henry (.303) – this single shot breech loaded rifle with a count of 400 rounds per weapon was no match to the assaulting thousands, but the men displayed better fire discipline and tactics. When nearly half the outpost were either dead or wounded during the first six hours of fighting, Havildar Ishar Singh ordered his men to leave the loop-holed battlements and get down to the main quartering block for hand-to-hand battle.

Their last message on the heliograph was : “We have been overrun, but will not surrender. Request permission to close down communication.” The permission was granted, the remaining men kept fighting for another few hours before making the supreme sacrifice. Their heroic last stand accounted for almost 450 men of the enemy either dead or wounded.

To recognize their brave act, all 21 soldiers were posthumously awarded the “Indian Order of Merit (IOM)”, which was the highest and the only gallantry medal then available to native Indian soldiers. Saragarhi is also recognised among the top eight stories of collective bravery by UNESCO. Recent research has revealed that in addition Karam Daad, another local NCE also died fighting with the 21 valiant Sikh soldiers. Some of the tribal attackers (who knew the two local NCEs) asked them to escape but both preferred to die fighting alongside their Sikh breathen.
