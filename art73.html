Building a Tradition of Valour

Title: Building a Tradition of Valour
Category: Building a Tradition of Valour
Artist: Sikh Regimental Centre
Year: Historical
Religious faith gives the Sikh soldier confidence in adversity and reinforces loyalty and honour. British officers who first fought the Sikhs and later served beside them repeatedly praised their steadiness under fire, eagerness in attack and refusal to abandon the field. A.H. Bingley wrote in 1918 that never turning one's back on the enemy formed part of the Sikh soldier's creed, while Lepel Griffin considered him equal to any soldier in the world.

After the First Anglo-Sikh War, the British were deeply impressed by Sikh resistance and began raising Sikh regiments on 1 August 1846, two and a half years before Punjab was annexed. Sikh troops thereafter became closely associated with the North-West Frontier, where forces under General Hari Singh Nalwa had earlier subdued Afghan and Pashtun resistance. Between 1850 and 1870, the British launched twenty-six frontier expeditions, and Sikh battalions formed a substantial part of the forces employed. By the turn of the twentieth century, they had received fifteen Battle Honours, two Victoria Crosses and numerous other gallantry distinctions.

Although Sikhs formed less than six per cent of Punjab's population, they constituted between one-fifth and almost one-half of the Indian Army from 1862 to 1914. At the outbreak of the First World War, 33,000 Sikhs were serving. From 1 August 1914 to 30 November 1918, 88,925 Sikh combatants were enlisted. Across the two World Wars, 83,005 Sikh soldiers were killed and 109,045 wounded, while Sikh units earned eight Theatre Honours and fifty-two Battle Honours.
