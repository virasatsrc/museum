Title: Burma Campaign (1941–45)
Category: Battle Gallery
Regiment: Sikh Regiment

The 1/11 SIKH (XIV) or 1st Battalion The Sikh Regiment was mobilized for war in February 1942, and moved to Burma by March 1942, where they stayed in operations for over three years, till August 1945. On 03 February 1944, the Japanese launched their assault on the 7th Division. The Allied plan was to advance up to Japanese positions in the tunnels. As part of this offensive, the 5th Division on the left was to take Razabil, while the 7th Division (of which 1/11 SIKH was part) was to take Buthidaung and then attack Letwedet. The brunt of the attack fell on the 89th Brigade and Charlie Company of 1/11 SIKH. On 06 February, the personnel withdrew to what famously came to be known as the Battle of Admin Box. The Japanese shortly captured Ngakyedauk Pass on 07 February, and tried to batter the besieged Division into submission, but the troops stood firm everywhere. Some of the severe fighting took place around the Admin Box. In this melee, Charlie Company fought a stubborn defensive battle to protect the Headquarters of 89th Brigade, and also defended the guns.

Bravo Company carried out raids and offensive patrolling. Eventually, the Japanese offensive petered out, and the scattered enemy in the area were steadily mopped up. By March 1944, 1/11 SIKH was back with 33th Brigade, now as an Integrated Battalion and its full combat potential was ready to be exploited. They were tasked to capture Buthidaung and the surrounding area, leading the advance. In the Battle for Buthidaung, one of the hill features, which later came to be known as India Hill, was left unoccupied. The Japanese were quick to exploit this. Characteristically, they dug in fast and began to interfere with troops on the neighbouring feature.

Charlie Company Commander, Major John Brough, was ordered to clear the enemy from India Hill. He deputed Jemadar Mehar Singh's platoon to capture it. As part of this platoon, on 12 March, Naik Nand Singh displayed outstanding valour and determination of an extremely high order. The only possible approach to the hill followed a narrow track, leading to the enemy position. Along this track, Naik Nand Singh led his section. Reaching the crest, the section came under heavy Machine Gun and rifle fire, and every man was knocked over, either being killed or wounded. Nonetheless, Naik Nand Singh dashed forward alone, under intense fire, at point blank range, and killed its two occupants with the bayonet, thus capturing the trench. Not far was another trench.

Under continuous heavy fire, Naik Nand Singh jumped up and charged on it. He was again wounded by grenade blast and knocked down, but he got up and hurled himself into the trench, again killing both occupants with the bayonet. He moved on further and captured a third trench, still single handed. With the capture of this third trench, the enemy fire died away. Naik Nand Singh's encounter had taken little time. The remainder of the platoon, checked for the moment by the sudden heavy fire, now moved up and captured the remainder of the position, killing with bayonets and grenades, 37 out of the 40 Japanese, who had been holding it. Nand Singh's part in this brilliant action, his splendid resolution and utter disregard for his own life were fittingly recognized by an immediate award of the Victoria Cross.

The 89th Brigade, of which 1/11 SIKH was a part, was detailed to carry out the subsidiary crossing of the Irrawaddy at Pagan. They were to hold the Division's firm base at Pauk, where the assaulting force was to assemble and carry out final rehearsals. In conformity with the plan, the 89th Brigade on an all-pack basis with Mountain Artillery, was sent off on a left hook, South of Gangaw, between Myotha and Chindwin. Gangaw fell in the first week of January 1945. 1/11 SIKH, as part of 89th Brigade, moved South to cut off the road from tilinto Pauk. On 29 January, the Battalion captured Sinthi and helped construct an airstrip which served as an airhead for the Irrawaddy crossing. On 05 February, the battalion captured Myit Chay. It earned the distinction of becoming the first troops to reach the Irrawaddy River, South of Mandalay. The crossing of the Irrawaddy at Nyaung-U was the longest opposed river crossing attempted in any theatre of the Second World War.

The river at this point was over 2000 yards wide and fairly fast flowing. The Southern Bank has sheer 100 feet cliffs, while the Northern Bank, from where the assault was being launched, consisted of paddy fields, devoid of cover, and was just a few feet above flood level. The roads serving the area were deeply rutted, and every vehicle raised a great column of dust clearly visible by day from the South Bank, thus not leaving much chance for surprise. Added to this, on the night of the crossing, there were strong winds and the water was churned. 1/11 SIKH was already across and located at Pagan. It was given the responsibility of intensive patrolling of the area. During these operations, the Battalion had two major brushes with the Japanese. In the first instance, on 11 November, two of their patrols were ambushed.

The Sikhs made a determined charge, during which most of the 150 Japanese facing them were either killed or routed. The Sikhs themselves suffered only 15 casualties. In the second case, on 05 May, the Vanguard Company of 1/11 SIKH, leading the 89th Brigade, was confronted with a similar force of Japanese. The Sikhs charged with blazing Bren Guns, and scattered the Japanese, killing 21 and taking one prisoner.Thereafter, there was a pause in battle. However, in the last major act, the Battalion, effectively blocked two important routes through which the Japanese had concentrated for a break through,i.e., Yenan and Shan.
