THE KHALSA ARMY

The years following Banda Bahadur's death were dire for the Sikhs, until 1734 when Kapur Singh (1697-1753), a battle-hardened Sikh guerilla fighter, organized the community into 'dals', or groups - collectively known as the Dal Khalsa, to offer armed resistance. Kapur Singh (his name now prefixed with the title 'Nawab') along with his close associate, Jassa Singh Ahluwalia (1718-1783) reorganized the Khalsa Army and utilized the period of Mughal-Afghan Conflict to consolidate itself. The Dal Khalsa was divided into twelve misls, which were empowered to undertake independent military actions, under a central authority.

The Khalsa Army slowly gave up its defensive posture and became offensive, harassing and plundering the armies of Nadir Shah and Ahmad Shah Abdali. The Sikhs inflicted severe damages to the booty-laden and battle-weary Afghan Army after the Third Battle of Panipat, which helped in extending and consolidating the Sikhs' hold over Punjab. Their secret of success was mobility and 'fight-run-fight' guerilla strategy. By 1769, the Mughal administration in Punjab was considerably weakened by repeated Afghan invasions and Sikh consolidation, which created a power vacuum filled by the Sikh misls who further expanded their territories.

The 'Khalsa' was truly a people's army, composed entirely of volunteers drawn from all sections of the society. Their basis of discipline was each soldier's inner conviction and his sense of dedication to the cause. The commanders were elected leaders who strove to carry their followers with them in a democratic manner. In these trying years, leaders like Nawab Kapur Singh and Jassa Singh Ahluwalia not only built up the Khalsa Army but also won back the confidence of the Muslim peasantry in Punjab, thus laying the foundation of the future secular kingdom of Lahore.
